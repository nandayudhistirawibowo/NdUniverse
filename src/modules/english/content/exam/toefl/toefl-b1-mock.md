    energy splits water molecules into oxygen and hydrogen. The oxygen is released into the air, while
    the hydrogen combines with carbon dioxide to form glucose, a sugar that fuels the plant’s growth.
    Two main stages occur: the light‑dependent reactions and the Calvin cycle. In the light‑dependent
    stage, light energy is converted into chemical energy in the form of ATP and NADPH. These molecules
    then power the Calvin cycle, where carbon dioxide is fixed into glucose. Temperature, light intensity,
    and carbon dioxide levels all affect the rate of photosynthesis. For example, on a bright, warm day
    with plenty of CO₂, plants photosynthesize faster than on a cloudy, cool day. That’s why greenhouse
    growers often control these factors to maximize crop yields.'
